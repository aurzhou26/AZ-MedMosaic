You're halfway through a run, feeling great, when suddenly a sharp pain appears beneath your ribs. You try to ignore it, but every step seems to make it worse.

You've encountered the infamous side stitch.

Medically known as **exercise-related transient abdominal pain (ETAP)**, a side stitch is a common phenomenon experienced during physical activity, especially running, swimming, and other exercises involving repetitive torso movement.

But what exactly causes that sensation?

Surprisingly, scientists still haven't reached a definitive answer.

## What Is a Side Stitch?

A side stitch is a localized abdominal pain that commonly occurs during exercise.

It is often described as sharp, stabbing, cramping, or pulling, usually around the sides of the abdomen or just beneath the rib cage.

The pain can occur on either side of the body and sometimes appears in the central abdomen.

Unlike delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), which develops after exercise due to muscle stress, ETAP typically occurs during physical activity and improves when the activity slows or stops.

## Theory 1: Irritation of the Peritoneum

One of the leading explanations involves a structure called the **peritoneum**.

The peritoneum is a thin, slippery membrane that lines the abdominal cavity and covers many of the internal organs.

It consists of two main layers:

- **Parietal peritoneum:** Lines the inside of the abdominal wall.
- **Visceral peritoneum:** Covers the surfaces of many abdominal organs.

Between these layers is a small amount of lubricating fluid that allows organs to move smoothly against one another.

During running, your torso repeatedly moves up and down. Researchers have proposed that this repetitive movement, along with changes in abdominal pressure, may increase friction or irritation between the peritoneal layers.

Because the parietal peritoneum contains sensory nerve endings capable of detecting pain, irritation could potentially produce the sharp, localized sensation associated with a side stitch.

This is one of the more widely discussed explanations for ETAP, although it has not been conclusively proven.

## Theory 2: Your Diaphragm Might Be Involved

The **diaphragm** is a dome-shaped skeletal muscle separating the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity.

It is also the primary muscle responsible for breathing.

When you inhale, the diaphragm contracts and moves downward, increasing the volume of the thoracic cavity and allowing air to enter the lungs.

When you exhale, it relaxes and moves upward.

During running, your respiratory rate increases to meet your muscles' growing demand for oxygen.

An older theory suggested that side stitches were caused by **diaphragmatic ischemia**, meaning the diaphragm wasn't receiving enough oxygenated blood to function comfortably.

However, research has challenged this explanation.

ETAP can occur during activities that don't heavily challenge the respiratory system, and the location of the pain doesn't always correspond to the diaphragm.

Although breathing mechanics may influence discomfort, insufficient oxygen supply to the diaphragm is no longer considered a convincing explanation for most side stitches.

## Theory 3: Your Digestive System Is Getting Involved

Ever gone for a run immediately after eating a large meal?

If so, you've probably discovered that your stomach isn't particularly enthusiastic about the idea.

During exercise, the body redistributes blood flow toward active skeletal muscles to support their increased metabolic demands.

At the same time, digestion involves the gastrointestinal tract, which requires blood flow and coordinated muscular activity.

Eating or drinking large quantities shortly before exercise can increase abdominal fullness and potentially contribute to discomfort.

Researchers have found that consuming food or beverages before exercise, particularly large volumes of certain concentrated drinks, can increase the likelihood of ETAP.

However, side stitches can also occur on an empty stomach, meaning digestion alone cannot explain every case.

## Theory 4: Repetitive Movement and Biomechanics

Running involves more than just your legs.

Your abdominal muscles, diaphragm, spine, and pelvic structures all work together to stabilize your torso.

With every stride, the abdominal organs experience repetitive movement while surrounding muscles maintain posture and balance.

Researchers have proposed that mechanical stress on tissues within the abdomen may contribute to ETAP.

Posture may also play a role. Some studies have identified associations between spinal posture and susceptibility to side stitches, although the relationship is not fully understood.

This could help explain why certain activities involving repeated torso movement seem particularly likely to trigger the pain.

## Why Does the Pain Sometimes Travel to Your Shoulder?

Here's where things get especially interesting.

Some people experiencing ETAP also report discomfort near the tip of their shoulder.

This may involve a neurological phenomenon called **referred pain**.

Referred pain occurs when discomfort originating in one anatomical location is perceived somewhere else.

The **phrenic nerve**, which originates primarily from spinal nerve roots C3-C5, supplies motor innervation to the diaphragm and sensory innervation to parts of the diaphragm-associated membranes.

These spinal segments also share sensory connections with regions near the shoulder.

As a result, irritation involving certain diaphragmatic tissues can sometimes produce pain perceived near the shoulder.

This observation is one reason researchers have investigated irritation of the parietal peritoneum as a possible mechanism behind ETAP.

## Why Are Some People More Prone to Side Stitches?

Not everyone experiences side stitches equally.

Research suggests that ETAP is reported more frequently among younger individuals, although people of any age can experience it.

Other possible contributing factors include:

- Eating a large meal shortly before exercise
- Consuming large amounts of fluid immediately before activity
- Drinking highly concentrated sugary beverages
- Repetitive torso movement
- Individual differences in posture and anatomy
- Certain exercise intensities or movement patterns

Interestingly, being physically fit doesn't guarantee that you'll never experience a side stitch.

Even experienced athletes can develop ETAP.

## How Can You Make a Side Stitch Go Away?

Although there isn't one scientifically proven treatment that works for everyone, several simple strategies may help.

### 1. Slow Down or Take a Break

Reducing exercise intensity can help relieve the discomfort.

For many people, the pain gradually disappears after slowing down, walking, or stopping the activity.

### 2. Focus on Controlled Breathing

Try taking slow, comfortable breaths rather than rapid, shallow ones.

Although research hasn't conclusively established that breathing techniques eliminate ETAP, some runners find that controlled breathing helps them manage the discomfort.

### 3. Apply Gentle Pressure

Some people find temporary relief by gently pressing against the painful area.

The precise mechanism isn't established, but pressure may alter how the discomfort is perceived.

### 4. Adjust Your Posture

Straightening your torso or gently stretching the affected side may help some people.

Avoid forcing a stretch if it increases the pain.

## How Can You Prevent Side Stitches?

Preventing ETAP often involves experimenting with exercise timing and habits.

### Give Yourself Time After Eating

Avoid running immediately after a large meal.

Some sports medicine recommendations suggest allowing approximately two to three hours after a substantial meal before vigorous exercise, although individual tolerance varies.

### Avoid Drinking Large Amounts All at Once

Staying hydrated is important, especially during physical activity.

However, consuming a large volume of liquid immediately before running may increase abdominal discomfort.

Instead, maintain normal hydration throughout the day and drink according to your needs.

### Warm Up Before Running

Gradually increasing exercise intensity allows your cardiovascular and respiratory systems to adjust to physical activity.

While warming up has many benefits, evidence that it specifically prevents ETAP remains limited.

### Build Core Strength and Postural Control

Your core muscles help stabilize your trunk during movement.

Strengthening the abdominal and back muscles may improve running mechanics, although research has not established core training as a guaranteed way to prevent side stitches.

## When Is Abdominal Pain More Than a Side Stitch?

A typical side stitch appears during exercise and improves when the activity slows or stops.

However, not every episode of abdominal pain during exercise is ETAP.

Seek medical attention if pain is severe, persistent, repeatedly occurs without a clear explanation, or is accompanied by symptoms such as:

- Fainting or significant dizziness
- Chest pain
- Difficulty breathing
- Fever
- Repeated vomiting
- Significant abdominal tenderness

Sudden severe abdominal pain or pain accompanied by fainting or serious breathing difficulties warrants emergency medical evaluation.

## The Takeaway

That sharp pain beneath your ribs during a run is more scientifically mysterious than it seems.

Exercise-related transient abdominal pain may involve irritation of the parietal peritoneum, repetitive movement of abdominal structures, gastrointestinal factors, and interactions between the diaphragm and surrounding tissues.

Although the exact mechanism remains uncertain, researchers have identified several factors that may influence when side stitches occur.

Fortunately, typical side stitches are temporary and often improve with reduced exercise intensity.

So the next time you're running and suddenly feel that familiar stabbing sensation, remember that your body isn't necessarily telling you that you're out of shape.

You might just be experiencing one of exercise physiology's surprisingly unresolved mysteries.
