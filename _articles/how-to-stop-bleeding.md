Cuts and scrapes happen all the time, and most minor bleeding can be controlled with basic first aid. The main goals are to control blood loss, protect the wound, and recognize when bleeding has become an emergency.

## 1. Check How Serious the Bleeding Is

Before treating the wound, quickly assess the situation.

Minor bleeding from small cuts and scrapes will usually slow with direct pressure.

However, **call 911 immediately** for life-threatening bleeding, such as blood that is rapidly or continuously flowing, a large amount of blood loss, or bleeding accompanied by weakness, pale or clammy skin, confusion, or decreasing responsiveness.

Severe blood loss can eventually cause **hemorrhagic shock**, where there is no longer enough circulating blood to adequately deliver oxygen to the body's tissues.

## 2. Apply Direct Pressure

For external bleeding, one of the most important first-aid actions is firm, continuous **direct pressure**.

Place sterile gauze or a clean cloth directly over the wound and press firmly.

Pressure physically compresses damaged blood vessels and reduces blood loss while the body's natural **hemostatic system** begins forming a blood clot.

Avoid repeatedly lifting the dressing to see if the bleeding has stopped. Doing so can disturb the developing clot.

If blood soaks through the material, continue applying pressure rather than repeatedly removing the original dressing.

## 3. Once Minor Bleeding Stops, Clean the Wound

For a small wound that has stopped bleeding, rinse it gently with clean running water.

Clean the surrounding skin with soap and water and remove easily accessible dirt or debris.

Avoid routinely pouring hydrogen peroxide or rubbing alcohol directly into the wound. Although these substances can kill microorganisms, they can also irritate healthy cells involved in tissue repair.

## 4. Cover and Protect It

Cover the cleaned wound with a sterile bandage or dressing.

This protects the damaged tissue from dirt and microorganisms while the skin begins repairing itself.

Replace the dressing if it becomes dirty, wet, or loose.

## What If Something Is Stuck in the Wound?

If a large object is embedded in a wound, **don't pull it out yourself**.

Removing an embedded object could cause additional tissue damage or suddenly worsen bleeding.

Get emergency medical help and avoid pressing directly down on the object.

## When Would You Use a Tourniquet?

A **tourniquet** is an emergency device used to control life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg.

It isn't meant for ordinary cuts.

Current American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidance recommends direct manual pressure initially for severe external bleeding and using a manufactured tourniquet as soon as possible for life-threatening extremity bleeding that isn't controlled by direct pressure.

A tourniquet works by applying strong circumferential pressure around the limb, compressing the underlying blood vessels and greatly reducing or stopping blood flow to the injured area.

### Using a Commercial Tourniquet

If someone has life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg, call 911 or have someone else call while first aid is being provided.

Use a commercially manufactured tourniquet according to its instructions and your training. In general, it is positioned on the injured limb between the wound and the person's torso, rather than directly over the wound or a joint.

The device is then tightened until the life-threatening bleeding stops.

Once an effective tourniquet has been applied, **do not loosen or remove it yourself** while waiting for emergency medical professionals. EMS personnel can determine how and when it should be managed.

If possible, note the approximate time the tourniquet was applied and communicate that information to emergency responders.

Tourniquets can be uncomfortable or painful when tightened effectively. The priority in a life-threatening hemorrhage is controlling the blood loss.

### Why Training Matters

Tourniquets are extremely valuable emergency tools, but reading about them isn't the same as practicing with one.

Courses such as **STOP THE BLEED®**, American Red Cross first-aid training, and other certified programs allow people to practice recognizing life-threatening hemorrhage, applying direct pressure, and correctly using commercial tourniquets on training equipment.

These are skills that are much easier to perform during an emergency if you've physically practiced them beforehand.

## What About Bleeding That Isn't on an Arm or Leg?

A tourniquet cannot be placed around areas such as the neck, chest, abdomen, or certain junctional areas of the body.

For severe external bleeding where a tourniquet isn't appropriate, continue firm direct pressure and follow instructions from the 911 dispatcher while emergency medical help is coming.

Certified bleeding-control courses may also teach techniques such as wound packing for certain severe wounds.

## Quick Reminder

**For an ordinary wound:**

Apply direct pressure → Keep pressure on → Clean once bleeding stops → Cover and protect.

**For life-threatening bleeding:**

Call 911 → Apply firm direct pressure → For uncontrolled life-threatening arm or leg bleeding, use a commercial tourniquet if available and you know how → Keep monitoring the person until EMS arrives.

The most important skill isn't identifying exactly which blood vessel was injured. It's recognizing when bleeding is severe and taking action quickly.
