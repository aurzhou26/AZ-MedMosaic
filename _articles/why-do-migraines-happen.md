Most people have experienced a headache. But a migraine is much more than head pain.

Migraines are complex neurological disorders that can affect vision, balance, speech, concentration, mood, and even digestion. For some people, the pain is so severe that everyday activities like walking, reading, or looking at a phone screen become nearly impossible.

Researchers estimate that over one billion people worldwide experience migraines, making them one of the most common neurological disorders. Yet despite how common they are, scientists are still uncovering exactly why they happen.

The good news? We understand much more about migraines today than we did even a decade ago.

## A Migraine Starts in the Brain — Not the Blood Vessels

For many years, scientists believed migraines were simply caused by blood vessels in the brain expanding and contracting.

While blood vessels do play a role, we now know that migraines are primarily a disorder of the nervous system involving abnormal communication between neurons, blood vessels, and inflammatory molecules.

Rather than being "just a headache," a migraine is a cascade of neurological events involving multiple brain regions and signaling pathways.

## The Trigeminal Nerve

One of the most important structures involved is the **trigeminal nerve** (cranial nerve V) — the largest sensory nerve of the face.

This nerve carries pain signals from the:

- Face
- Eyes
- Sinuses
- Meninges (the protective membranes surrounding the brain)

During a migraine attack, the trigeminal nerve becomes **hyperexcitable**, meaning it fires pain signals more easily than it normally should.

As it activates, nerve endings release substances called **neuropeptides**, including one of the most important molecules in migraine research:

**Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP)**.

## What Is CGRP?

CGRP is a naturally occurring neuropeptide that plays a major role in migraine attacks.

When released, CGRP can:

- Dilate blood vessels surrounding the brain
- Increase inflammation around pain-sensitive tissues
- Amplify pain signaling
- Sensitize nearby nerve endings

This process is called **neurogenic inflammation**.

Importantly, the inflammation occurring during migraines is different from an infection. Instead of bacteria or viruses causing inflammation, the nervous system itself releases chemicals that make pain pathways more sensitive.

The discovery of CGRP has transformed migraine treatment, leading to an entirely new class of medications designed specifically to block its effects.

## Why Does Everything Feel Worse?

One hallmark of migraines is **sensitization**.

As the nervous system becomes increasingly activated, neurons begin responding more strongly to normal stimuli.

This explains why many people become sensitive to:

- Bright lights (photophobia)
- Loud sounds (phonophobia)
- Strong smells (osmophobia)
- Touching the scalp
- Physical activity

Even normal sensory input may feel overwhelming because the brain has essentially turned up its "volume knob."

## Migraine Aura

About one in three people with migraines experience an **aura** before or during an attack.

An aura may include:

- Flashing lights
- Zigzag lines
- Blind spots
- Numbness or tingling
- Difficulty speaking
- Temporary weakness (less commonly)

These symptoms are thought to result from a phenomenon called **cortical spreading depression (CSD)**.

Despite its name, CSD is not depression in the emotional sense.

Instead, it is a slow-moving wave of neuronal depolarization followed by temporary suppression of brain activity that travels across the cerebral cortex.

As this electrical wave moves through areas responsible for vision, sensation, or language, it temporarily disrupts their normal function, producing the symptoms of an aura.

## Why Do Migraines Make You Nauseous?

Many people with migraines experience nausea, vomiting, or loss of appetite.

This happens because migraines don't affect only pain pathways — they also involve regions such as the:

- Brainstem
- Hypothalamus
- Area postrema (the brain's "vomiting center")

These regions communicate with the gastrointestinal system through the **autonomic nervous system**, explaining why migraines often affect digestion as well as the head.

Some people even develop **gastroparesis**, meaning the stomach temporarily empties more slowly during an attack. This can make oral medications take longer to work.

## Why Do Migraines Happen in the First Place?

Scientists believe migraines result from a combination of **genetics** and **environmental triggers**.

Having one parent with migraines significantly increases your own risk.

However, genes alone usually aren't enough. Many migraine attacks occur when multiple triggers combine.

Common triggers include:

- Sleep deprivation or oversleeping
- Emotional stress
- Hormonal fluctuations (particularly changes in estrogen)
- Dehydration
- Skipping meals
- Alcohol
- Certain foods for some individuals
- Bright or flashing lights
- Strong odors
- Weather or barometric pressure changes

Not everyone has the same triggers, which is why keeping a migraine diary can help identify personal patterns.

## Four Phases of a Migraine

Migraines often develop in predictable stages.

### 1. Prodrome (Hours to Days Before)

Some people notice subtle warning signs before the headache begins, including:

- Fatigue
- Mood changes
- Food cravings
- Neck stiffness
- Frequent yawning
- Difficulty concentrating

These symptoms likely involve the **hypothalamus**, a brain region that regulates sleep, appetite, hormones, and circadian rhythms.

### 2. Aura (Not Everyone Experiences This)

Temporary neurological symptoms caused by cortical spreading depression.

### 3. Headache Phase

Typically characterized by:

- Moderate to severe throbbing pain
- Usually affecting one side of the head (but not always)
- Worsening with physical activity
- Nausea or vomiting
- Light and sound sensitivity

This phase usually lasts **4 to 72 hours** if untreated.

### 4. Postdrome

Even after the headache resolves, many people experience:

- Fatigue
- Brain fog
- Difficulty concentrating
- Dizziness
- Feeling mentally "drained"

Some describe this as a "migraine hangover."

## Can You Prevent Migraines?

While migraines can't always be prevented, many people can reduce how often they occur.

### Stay Hydrated

Even mild dehydration may trigger migraines in susceptible individuals.

### Prioritize Sleep

Aim for a consistent sleep schedule. Going to bed and waking up at similar times each day helps regulate the brain's internal clock.

### Don't Skip Meals

Large swings in blood sugar may trigger attacks in some people.

### Manage Stress

Stress doesn't simply "cause headaches." It alters hormone levels, autonomic nervous system activity, and inflammatory signaling.

Helpful strategies include:

- Regular exercise
- Meditation
- Deep breathing
- Yoga
- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)

### Identify Personal Triggers

Keeping track of:

- Sleep
- Meals
- Weather
- Stress
- Hormonal changes
- Foods
- Migraine timing

can reveal patterns that aren't immediately obvious.

## What Treatments Are Available?

Treatment generally falls into two categories.

### Acute (Abortive) Treatments

These are taken once a migraine begins to stop or reduce an attack.

Examples include:

- NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen or naproxen)
- Acetaminophen
- Triptans (which activate serotonin receptors and reduce CGRP release)
- Gepants (CGRP receptor blockers)
- Ditans (serotonin receptor agonists without vasoconstriction)

The earlier these medications are taken during an attack, the more effective they tend to be.

### Preventive Treatments

For people with frequent or disabling migraines, doctors may recommend preventive therapies such as:

- Beta-blockers
- Certain anti-seizure medications
- Some antidepressants
- CGRP monoclonal antibodies
- OnabotulinumtoxinA (Botox®) for chronic migraine

Lifestyle modifications are often combined with these medications for the best results.

## The Takeaway

Migraines aren't simply the result of "blood vessels expanding" or "too much stress." They are complex neurological events involving the trigeminal nerve, CGRP, cortical spreading depression, altered sensory processing, and interactions between the brain, immune system, and blood vessels.

Although researchers are still uncovering every detail, our growing understanding of migraine biology has led to more targeted treatments than ever before. By identifying personal triggers, maintaining healthy lifestyle habits, and working with a healthcare provider when needed, many people can reduce both the frequency and severity of migraine attacks — and reclaim days that migraines might otherwise take away.
