You accidentally slam your elbow into the corner of a desk.

Suddenly, a bizarre combination of tingling, numbness, pain, and what feels suspiciously like electricity shoots from your elbow toward your hand.

You've hit your "funny bone." Except, your funny bone isn't actually a bone.

It's a nerve.

## Meet the Ulnar Nerve

The strange sensation you experience after hitting your funny bone comes primarily from the **ulnar nerve**, one of the major nerves supplying your forearm and hand.

The ulnar nerve originates from a network of nerves near the neck and shoulder called the **brachial plexus**. From there, it travels down the arm, passes behind the elbow, and continues into the hand.

It helps control some of the small muscles responsible for fine movements of your fingers and carries sensory information from parts of your hand, particularly around the little finger and part of the ring finger.

Most of this journey happens relatively safely underneath muscles and other tissues.

Then the nerve reaches your elbow, and suddenly, its protective packaging gets considerably worse.

## Why Is the Nerve So Vulnerable at Your Elbow?

Feel the hard bump on the inner side of your elbow? That bump is part of the humerus called the **medial epicondyle**.

As the ulnar nerve travels toward your forearm, it passes behind this area through a narrow region called the **cubital tunnel**.

Here, the nerve sits relatively close to the surface of your skin. There isn't much muscle or fatty tissue cushioning it from the outside world. So when your elbow meets the corner of a table, there may be very little between the impact and your ulnar nerve.

## Why Does It Feel Electric?

Nerves communicate using electrical and chemical signals.

Individual nerve cells maintain differences in electrical charge across their cell membranes. When sufficiently stimulated, they generate rapid electrical changes called **action potentials**.

Normally, sensory receptors detect things like pressure, temperature, or touch and send organized signals toward your central nervous system.

Smacking the exposed portion of the ulnar nerve is different. The mechanical impact suddenly stimulates many nerve fibers at once. Your brain receives an unusual burst of sensory information and tries to interpret it.

The result can feel like:

- Tingling
- Burning
- Pins and needles
- Numbness
- A sudden shock
- Pain

## Why Does the Feeling Shoot Down Your Arm?

This is probably the coolest part.

You hit your elbow. So why can you feel the sensation all the way down in your fingers?

Because the ulnar nerve doesn't exist only at your elbow. The nerve fibers passing through that location carry sensory information between your brain and areas farther down your arm and hand.

When you strike the nerve at the elbow, you can activate fibers that normally carry information from those distant areas.

Your brain interprets the incoming signals according to where those nerve fibers usually receive information from.

So instead of feeling everything exclusively at your elbow, you may experience tingling traveling down your forearm and into your hand.

It's almost like hitting one electrical cable and causing lights farther down the circuit to flicker.

## Why the Pinky and Ring Finger?

Try to remember where the tingling travels the next time you accidentally hit your funny bone.

You may notice that it seems particularly noticeable around your little finger and the adjacent half of your ring finger.

That's not random. Those regions receive much of their sensation through branches of the ulnar nerve. Different peripheral nerves are responsible for sensation in different regions of the hand.

For example, the **median nerve** supplies much of the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and part of the ring finger, while the ulnar nerve covers much of the remaining territory.

So when the ulnar nerve gets irritated at your elbow, the weird sensation follows its neurological map.

## So Why Is It Called the "Funny Bone"?

There are two commonly suggested explanations.

One is anatomical wordplay.

The upper-arm bone is called the **humerus**.

"Humerus" sounds like "humorous."

Funny → humorous → humerus.

Another explanation is simply that hitting the area produces a strange or "funny" sensation rather than ordinary pain.

The exact historical origin of the nickname isn't completely certain, and it may reflect a combination of both ideas.

## Why Doesn't Hitting Every Nerve Feel Like This?

Most peripheral nerves are better protected.

Your body generally routes important nerves beneath layers of:

- Skin
- Fat
- Fascia
- Muscle
- Connective tissue

These tissues help cushion nerves from ordinary impacts.

The ulnar nerve's superficial position around the elbow makes it unusually easy to compress directly against the underlying bone.

There are other places where nerves become relatively superficial, but the elbow happens to combine an exposed nerve with a body part humans regularly smash into furniture.

## What Happens After You Hit It?

Fortunately, an ordinary funny-bone hit usually causes only temporary nerve irritation.

Once the mechanical pressure disappears, the abnormal nerve firing settles down and the tingling gradually fades.

That explains why you might spend thirty seconds clutching your elbow and questioning reality before suddenly feeling completely normal again.

However, repeated or prolonged compression of the ulnar nerve is different.

## When the "Funny Bone" Isn't Funny Anymore

The ulnar nerve can sometimes become chronically compressed around the elbow, producing a condition called **cubital tunnel syndrome**.

This is somewhat similar in concept to carpal tunnel syndrome, except a different nerve is affected at a different anatomical location.

Symptoms can include:

- Recurrent numbness or tingling in the little and ring fingers
- Hand weakness
- Difficulty with fine finger movements
- Symptoms that worsen when the elbow remains bent
- Aching around the inner elbow

Someone who repeatedly experiences these symptoms without recently hitting their elbow should tell a parent/guardian and consider discussing them with a healthcare professional.

## Your Ulnar Nerve Does More Than Create Pain

The ulnar nerve has extremely important functions.

It controls several muscles involved in fine hand movements.

Try spreading your fingers apart.

Many of the small **interosseous muscles** responsible for that movement receive motor signals from the ulnar nerve.

It also contributes to movements involving your little finger and grip.

That means the same nerve responsible for the world's least funny "funny bone" sensation is also helping you type, grip objects, play instruments, draw, and perform countless precise movements every day.

## The Bottom Line

Your funny bone isn't a bone at all.

When you hit that particular part of your elbow, you're usually compressing or striking the ulnar nerve as it passes close to the surface behind the medial epicondyle of the humerus.

The impact causes abnormal nerve signals that your brain interprets as a strange mixture of pain, tingling, numbness, and electrical shock.

And because those nerve fibers continue into your hand, the sensation can shoot all the way into your ring and little fingers.

So the next time your elbow loses a fight with a table and your entire arm seems to short-circuit, remember: you accidentally sent a very enthusiastic message directly through your ulnar nerve.
