You're sitting in class, trying your absolute hardest not to look tired, when a huge yawn hits. Your mouth opens, your eyes squint shut, and suddenly they're filled with tears.

You aren't crying. Nothing got in your eye. So why does yawning sometimes make you tear up?

The answer involves the tiny system responsible for producing, spreading, and draining your tears: the **lacrimal system**.

## Where Do Tears Actually Come From?

Your eyes are constantly covered by a thin layer of fluid called the **tear film**.

Tears aren't only produced when you cry. They are continuously helping to:

- Lubricate the surface of the eye
- Protect the cornea
- Wash away small particles
- Reduce friction from blinking
- Defend against microorganisms

One major source of the watery portion of your tears is the **lacrimal gland**, located above and toward the outer side of each eye.

When you blink, your eyelids spread tears across the cornea like tiny windshield wipers.

But those tears eventually need somewhere to go.

## Your Eyes Have Their Own Drainage System

Near the inner corner of each eyelid are tiny openings called the **lacrimal puncta**.

Normally, tears drain through these openings into small channels called the **canaliculi**. From there, they travel into the **lacrimal sac** and eventually through the **nasolacrimal duct** into the nasal cavity.

The pathway looks roughly like this:

> Tear film → lacrimal puncta → canaliculi → lacrimal sac → nasolacrimal duct → nose

This connection between your eyes and nose also helps explain why your nose can start running when you cry. Some of those tears are literally draining into your nasal cavity.

## What Changes When You Yawn?

Yawning activates and stretches a surprising number of facial muscles.

Your jaw opens widely, your cheeks move, and muscles surrounding your eyes contract. One of these is the **orbicularis oculi**, the circular muscle responsible for closing the eyelids.

That's why your eyes often squint or completely close during a big yawn.

These facial movements can temporarily alter how tears are distributed and drained around the eye.

If tears temporarily accumulate faster than they can drain through the lacrimal system, they collect along the eyelid.

The result is that familiar watery-eyed look after a huge yawn.

## Are Your Lacrimal Glands Being "Squeezed"?

You may have heard that yawning causes tears because your facial muscles simply squeeze the lacrimal glands like little water balloons.

That's an oversimplification.

The lacrimal gland sits in the **superolateral portion of the orbit**, the upper outer area surrounding the eye, and tear production is controlled largely through neural pathways rather than simply by mechanically crushing the gland.

Yawning involves coordinated changes in facial muscles, eyelid position, tear distribution, and possibly autonomic nervous system activity. Together, these can make tears noticeably collect on the surface of the eye.

So the explanation is less "you squeezed the tear gland" and more "yawning temporarily changed the mechanics and neural regulation of your lacrimal system."

## The Nervous System Controls Tear Production Too

The lacrimal glands are influenced by the **autonomic nervous system**, the part of your nervous system that regulates processes you usually don't consciously control.

The **parasympathetic nervous system** is especially important for lacrimal secretion.

Signals associated with tear production ultimately travel through pathways involving the **facial nerve**, or **cranial nerve VII**.

This is why tear production isn't simply a mechanical process. It's a neurophysiological one.

Your brain, cranial nerves, glands, muscles, and drainage system all have to work together just to maintain that tiny layer of moisture covering your eyes.

## Why Don't My Eyes Water Every Time I Yawn?

Not everyone tears up with every yawn.

How noticeable it is can depend on factors such as:

- How strongly your facial muscles contract
- How tightly your eyes close
- How much tear fluid is already present
- Individual differences in tear production
- How efficiently your tears are draining

Sometimes you may only notice a little extra moisture. Other times, an actual tear may roll down your cheek.

Both can be completely normal.

## Why Does Your Nose Run When You Cry?

This is where the anatomy comes full circle.

When you're producing lots of tears, not all of them spill over your eyelids.

Many enter the lacrimal puncta and travel through the nasolacrimal ducts into your nose.

Once that extra fluid reaches the nasal cavity, it mixes with mucus.

That's why intense crying can leave you simultaneously wiping your eyes and blowing your nose.

Your eyes and nose are literally connected by a drainage system.

## What If My Eyes Water Constantly?

Occasional watering during yawning, laughing, crying, or exposure to wind is normal.

Constant or excessive tearing is called **epiphora**.

Epiphora can have several possible causes, including:

- Eye irritation
- Allergies
- Dry-eye disease
- Infection
- Eyelid abnormalities
- Problems with the tear drainage system
- Blockage of the nasolacrimal duct

Strangely enough, dry eyes can sometimes cause watery eyes. If the ocular surface becomes irritated from inadequate lubrication, the nervous system may respond by producing a burst of reflex tears.

If excessive tearing is persistent, painful, associated with vision changes, or occurs alongside significant redness or swelling, it should be evaluated by an eye-care professional.

## The Takeaway

Those watery eyes after a massive yawn aren't random.

When you yawn, muscles around your eyes and face contract, your eyelids squeeze shut, and the normal movement and drainage of tears can temporarily change. At the same time, tear production itself is regulated by neural pathways involving the autonomic nervous system and cranial nerve VII.

If tears accumulate faster than your lacrimal drainage system clears them, they stay on the surface of your eyes.

So the next time you yawn and suddenly look like you're crying, you're actually watching your lacrimal system, facial muscles, cranial nerves, and tear-drainage anatomy interact in real time.

All because you were tired.
