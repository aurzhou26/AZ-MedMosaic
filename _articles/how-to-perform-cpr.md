Cardiac arrest occurs when the heart suddenly stops pumping blood effectively. Without oxygen-rich blood reaching the brain and other vital organs, permanent brain damage can begin within 4–6 minutes. CPR (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation) temporarily takes over the heart's job by manually circulating blood until emergency medical professionals can provide advanced care. Early CPR can double or even triple a person's chance of survival.

## Recognizing Cardiac Arrest

Someone may be in cardiac arrest if they:

- Suddenly collapse.
- Are unresponsive when you tap and shout.
- Are not breathing normally or are only gasping (agonal breathing).
- Have no detectable pulse if you are trained to check for one.

If you suspect cardiac arrest, begin CPR immediately.

## Step 1: Ensure the Scene is Safe

Before approaching, quickly check that there are no immediate dangers such as traffic, fire, electrical hazards, or unstable structures. Your safety is always the first priority.

## Step 2: Check for Responsiveness

Tap the person's shoulders firmly and loudly ask:

**"Are you okay?"**

If there is no response, quickly assess their breathing for no more than 10 seconds.

## Step 3: Call 911 and Get an AED

Call 911 immediately or instruct someone nearby to do so. If another person is available, have them locate an Automated External Defibrillator (AED). If you're alone with an adult, call 911 first (using speakerphone if possible), then begin CPR.

The sooner an AED is used, the greater the chance of restoring a normal heart rhythm.

## Step 4: Begin Chest Compressions

Place the heel of one hand in the center of the chest on the lower half of the sternum (breastbone). Place your second hand on top and interlock your fingers.

**Keep:**

- Arms straight.
- Shoulders directly above your hands.
- Your body weight over your hands rather than using arm strength alone.

**Perform compressions:**

- **Rate:** 100–120 compressions per minute.
- **Depth:** At least 2 inches (5 cm), but no deeper than 2.4 inches (6 cm) in adults.
- Allow complete chest recoil after every compression.
- Minimize interruptions as much as possible.

A helpful rhythm is the beat of "Stayin' Alive" by the Bee Gees or "Crazy in Love" by Beyoncé.

## Step 5: Rescue Breaths (If Trained)

If you have CPR training:

After every 30 chest compressions:

1. Open the airway using the head-tilt/chin-lift maneuver.
2. Pinch the person's nose closed.
3. Form a seal over their mouth.
4. Give 2 rescue breaths lasting about 1 second each.
5. Watch for visible chest rise.

If the chest doesn't rise, reposition the head and try again.

Continue cycles of:

- 30 compressions
- 2 breaths

If you are not trained or uncomfortable giving rescue breaths, perform **Hands-Only CPR** by continuously providing chest compressions until help arrives.

## Using an AED

As soon as an AED becomes available:

1. Turn the AED on.
2. Expose the person's chest.
3. Attach the electrode pads exactly as shown on the diagrams.
4. Ensure no one is touching the person while the AED analyzes the heart rhythm.
5. If instructed, deliver the shock.
6. Immediately resume CPR after the shock or when prompted.

Modern AEDs are designed to only deliver a shock if one is needed—they cannot accidentally shock someone whose heart doesn't require it.

## CPR for Children (Ages 1 to Puberty)

Children usually experience cardiac arrest because of breathing problems rather than primary heart disease, making rescue breaths especially important.

The overall process is the same:

1. Confirm the child is unresponsive.
2. Call 911.
3. Begin CPR immediately.

**Chest compressions:**

- Use one or two hands depending on the child's size.
- Compress approximately 2 inches (5 cm) deep, or about one-third the depth of the chest.
- Maintain a rate of 100–120 compressions per minute.

**Rescue breaths:**

- Give 30 compressions followed by 2 breaths if you are alone.
- If two trained rescuers are present, perform 15 compressions followed by 2 breaths.

## CPR for Infants (Under 1 Year)

Infant CPR requires a modified technique because their bones are much more delicate.

**Chest compressions:**

- Use two fingers placed just below the nipple line if you're the only rescuer.
- Compress approximately 1.5 inches (4 cm), or one-third the depth of the chest.
- Maintain a rate of 100–120 compressions per minute.

**If two trained rescuers are available:**

- Use the two-thumb encircling hands technique.
- Perform 15 compressions followed by 2 breaths.

## When Can You Stop CPR?

Continue CPR until:

- EMS personnel take over.
- The person begins breathing normally or shows obvious signs of life.
- An AED instructs you to stop.
- The scene becomes unsafe.
- You become physically unable to continue.

## Common CPR Mistakes

Avoid:

- Compressing too slowly.
- Compressing too shallowly.
- Leaning on the chest between compressions (preventing full recoil).
- Pausing compressions for long periods.
- Waiting for someone "more qualified" before starting CPR.

Remember: Immediate CPR performed by a bystander is almost always better than waiting. Even if your technique isn't perfect, your actions can keep oxygenated blood flowing to the brain and significantly improve someone's chance of survival.
