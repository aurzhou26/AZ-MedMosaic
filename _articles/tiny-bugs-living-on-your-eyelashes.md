Yes, you probably have tiny arachnid-like creatures living around your eyelashes right now.

Before you start aggressively washing your face — that's usually completely normal.

They're called **Demodex mites**, microscopic organisms that live in or around human hair follicles and sebaceous glands. Most adults carry them without ever noticing. They are tiny, generally around a fraction of a millimeter long, so you aren't going to catch one casually strolling across your eyelid in the mirror.

And despite their reputation as "eyelash bugs," Demodex aren't actually insects. They're arachnids, making them relatives of spiders, ticks, and other mites.

## Meet Your Microscopic Roommates

Humans are primarily associated with two species:

- **Demodex folliculorum** — Usually lives inside hair follicles, particularly around eyelashes and other facial hair.
- **Demodex brevis** — Tends to live deeper within sebaceous glands, which produce sebum, the oily substance that helps protect and moisturize your skin.

Their elongated bodies are perfectly adapted for squeezing into these tiny spaces.

So while you're going about your day, an entire microscopic ecosystem may be existing around your eyelashes without you noticing.

## What Are They Doing There?

Demodex mites aren't living on your eyelashes because eyelashes themselves are particularly delicious. They're attracted to the environment surrounding the follicle.

Hair follicles and nearby glands contain:

- Sebum
- Skin cells
- Cellular debris
- Lipids and other materials

These provide both shelter and nutrients for the mites.

For most people, Demodex exist as **commensal organisms** — organisms that live on or inside another organism without normally causing significant harm.

Your face is essentially providing free housing.

## Why Don't We Feel Them Moving?

Thankfully, Demodex mites are microscopic.

*D. folliculorum* is generally only about 0.3–0.4 millimeters long. Their movements are far too small to produce sensations your nervous system would normally recognize.

They also tend to avoid light and become more active in darkness. Meaning while you're sleeping, your microscopic roommates may be considerably more active.

You're welcome.

## How Do People Get Them?

Humans aren't necessarily born with large populations of Demodex.

Instead, mites can be transferred through close contact between people, particularly through prolonged skin-to-skin contact. Populations generally become more common as people get older.

Once established, they occupy follicles and glands where conditions allow them to survive.

This also means Demodex are incredibly widespread. Having them isn't a sign that someone is dirty or has poor hygiene. They're simply one component of the organisms associated with human skin.

## Wait… Our Skin Has an Ecosystem?

Absolutely.

Your skin hosts an enormous community of microorganisms collectively called the **skin microbiome**. It includes:

- Bacteria
- Fungi
- Viruses
- Microscopic mites

Different regions of your body create completely different habitats depending on moisture, temperature, oil production, and exposure to the environment. The oily environment around the face and eyelids happens to provide an excellent habitat for Demodex.

In ecological terms, your face is less like an empty surface and more like a microscopic neighborhood. Demodex just happen to be some of its strangest residents.

## Do Demodex Mites Hurt Us?

Usually, no.

Small populations can exist without producing symptoms, and many people never know they're there.

Problems can develop, however, when unusually large populations accumulate or when someone develops inflammation associated with them. High Demodex densities have been associated with conditions including **blepharitis**, which involves inflammation of the eyelids.

Possible symptoms can include:

- Itchy or irritated eyelids
- Redness
- Burning sensations
- Crusting around the eyelashes
- A feeling that something is in the eye

One characteristic finding associated with Demodex blepharitis is **collarettes** — small cylindrical deposits of debris that form around the base of eyelashes.

Having Demodex mites alone, however, does not mean you have blepharitis.

## Why Can Too Many Become a Problem?

Think of Demodex populations like residents of an ecosystem.

A small, stable population may cause no noticeable problems. But if the population increases significantly, the mites and associated debris can contribute to irritation and inflammatory responses around the follicles and eyelids.

Researchers continue to investigate exactly how Demodex interact with the immune system, skin microbiome, and inflammatory pathways.

This is an important distinction: Demodex aren't automatically "parasites attacking your eyes." The relationship between humans and Demodex is more complicated than simply good versus bad.

## Do You Need to Get Rid of Them?

If you have no symptoms, you generally don't need to wage war against your eyelashes.

Trying to sterilize your skin isn't realistic — or desirable. Your skin naturally supports many microscopic organisms. Normal facial and eyelid hygiene is usually sufficient.

If someone develops persistent eyelid itching, redness, crusting, swelling, eye irritation, or changes around the eyelashes, an optometrist or ophthalmologist can determine whether Demodex-associated blepharitis or another eye condition may be responsible.

Don't put harsh chemicals, essential oils, or household products near your eyes in an attempt to eliminate mites. The eye and surrounding tissues are extremely sensitive, and irritation or injury can be much worse than the mites themselves.

## A Microscopic Life Cycle

Like other mites, Demodex go through multiple developmental stages. Their life cycle includes:

**Egg → larva → nymph → adult**

Much of this cycle occurs within or around hair follicles and sebaceous glands. Adults reproduce and eventually die, while new generations continue occupying the same microscopic environment.

So a single eyelash follicle isn't necessarily just holding a hair — it can function as an entire habitat.

## Are They Only on Eyelashes?

No.

Demodex particularly favor oily regions of the face and can be found around areas such as:

- Eyebrows
- Forehead
- Nose
- Cheeks
- Chin
- External ear regions

They're simply especially interesting around eyelashes because follicles and associated glands provide suitable habitats, and because excessive populations there can contribute to recognizable eyelid conditions.

## The Bottom Line

Your body isn't just one organism. It's an ecosystem.

Among the bacteria, fungi, and other microscopic organisms living alongside us are Demodex mites — tiny relatives of spiders that can inhabit the follicles surrounding our eyelashes.

Most of the time, they live there without causing noticeable problems. Only when populations or inflammatory responses become abnormal are they likely to attract medical attention.

So yes, there may be microscopic arachnids living around your eyelashes. But instead of thinking, "There are bugs on my face," consider the stranger reality: even something as ordinary as an eyelash can contain an ecosystem invisible to the naked eye.
