You're about to give a presentation in front of your entire class. You've practiced your speech a hundred times, memorized every slide, and convinced yourself that you're completely prepared.

But then your name gets called.

Suddenly, your stomach drops. Your appetite disappears, your abdomen feels strangely tight, and you might even feel like you're about to throw up.

Your brain knows you're just giving a presentation, so why is your stomach acting like you're in danger?

The answer lies in one of the most fascinating communication networks in the human body: the **gut-brain axis**.

## Your Gut and Brain Are Constantly Talking

Although we usually associate thinking and emotions with the brain, the gastrointestinal tract has its own extensive network of neurons.

This network is called the **enteric nervous system (ENS)**.

Sometimes nicknamed the "second brain," the ENS contains hundreds of millions of neurons embedded within the walls of the digestive tract.

These neurons help regulate digestion, including the movement of food, secretion of digestive fluids, and coordination of intestinal muscle contractions.

However, your enteric nervous system doesn't operate completely independently of your brain.

Instead, the two communicate through the gut-brain axis, a bidirectional communication network involving the nervous system, hormones, immune signaling, and intestinal microorganisms.

Bidirectional means that signals travel in both directions.

Your brain can influence how your gut behaves, and your gut can send information back to your brain.

This explains why emotional experiences can produce real physical sensations in your abdomen.

## What Happens in Your Brain When You Get Anxious?

When you encounter something stressful, such as an upcoming exam, your brain begins evaluating the situation.

One important structure involved in detecting emotionally significant or potentially threatening situations is the **amygdala**.

The amygdala communicates with other brain regions, including the **hypothalamus**, which helps coordinate the body's stress response.

The hypothalamus can activate the **sympathetic nervous system**, a division of the autonomic nervous system responsible for preparing the body to respond to challenges.

This response is commonly called **fight-or-flight**.

During sympathetic activation, your body may experience:

- An increased heart rate
- Faster breathing
- Increased alertness
- Sweating
- Changes in digestive activity
- Muscle tension

These changes help your body prepare for a demanding situation.

Unfortunately, your nervous system doesn't always distinguish between physical danger and psychological stress in the way you might expect.

Even when you're perfectly safe, worrying about a test or presentation can trigger a noticeable physiological response.

## Why Does Your Stomach Suddenly Drop?

That strange sinking sensation is one of the most recognizable physical symptoms of anxiety.

But your stomach isn't literally falling.

Instead, anxiety changes the activity of your **autonomic nervous system**, which regulates involuntary functions such as heart rate, digestion, and blood vessel diameter.

The sympathetic nervous system can alter gastrointestinal motility, blood flow, and sensitivity.

**Gastrointestinal motility** refers to the coordinated contractions of smooth muscle that move food and other contents through the digestive tract.

When those patterns change, you may experience sensations of tightness, fluttering, nausea, or abdominal discomfort.

At the same time, anxiety can increase your awareness of internal bodily sensations.

This is called **interoception**: the nervous system's ability to detect and interpret signals originating from inside the body.

In other words, anxiety can both change what your gut is doing and make your brain more sensitive to those changes.

That's why the sensation can feel so intense even when nothing dangerous is happening in your digestive system.

## The Vagus Nerve

One of the most important communication pathways between your brain and gastrointestinal tract is the **vagus nerve**.

The vagus nerve, also known as **cranial nerve X**, is a major component of the parasympathetic nervous system.

It carries information between the brainstem and organs including the heart, lungs, stomach, and intestines.

Interestingly, much of the information traveling through the vagus nerve is sensory information moving from the body toward the brain.

These sensory signals help the brain monitor internal conditions, including gastrointestinal activity.

The vagus nerve also participates in regulating digestive functions such as stomach movement and secretion.

However, the vagus nerve isn't the only pathway involved in the gut-brain connection.

Signals also travel through sympathetic nerves, spinal sensory pathways, hormones, and immune mediators.

Together, these systems allow emotional states to influence digestive function.

## Why Does Anxiety Make You Feel Nauseous?

Nausea is another common gastrointestinal symptom associated with anxiety.

During a stressful situation, your brain can alter the normal coordination of stomach activity.

In some circumstances, stress can slow **gastric emptying**, which is the process by which the stomach transfers its contents into the small intestine.

When stomach movement changes, you may experience fullness, queasiness, or nausea.

Stress can also influence neural circuits in the brainstem involved in nausea and vomiting.

Additionally, heightened visceral sensitivity can make normal digestive sensations feel unusually uncomfortable.

**Visceral sensitivity** refers to how strongly the nervous system responds to sensations arising from internal organs.

When this sensitivity increases, ordinary stomach contractions or stretching may feel much more noticeable.

You can feel genuinely nauseated even though you aren't sick and haven't eaten anything unusual.

## Why Do You Suddenly Need to Use the Bathroom?

Have you ever been nervous before a major event and suddenly felt an urgent need to use the bathroom?

This happens because the stress response can influence different parts of the digestive tract in different ways.

While stress may slow some stomach functions, it can increase motility or urgency in parts of the colon.

The colon is responsible for absorbing water and moving waste toward the rectum.

Changes in intestinal contractions and sensitivity can create abdominal cramping, urgency, or loose stools.

This is why some people experience what is casually called a "nervous stomach."

It isn't simply your imagination. Your emotional state is influencing the activity of smooth muscle and sensory nerves within your gastrointestinal tract.

## What Does Cortisol Have to Do With It?

The sympathetic nervous system isn't the only system activated during stress.

Your body also uses a hormonal pathway called the **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis**, or **HPA axis**.

Here's how it works:

1. The hypothalamus releases **corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)**.
2. CRH stimulates the anterior pituitary gland to release **adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)**.
3. ACTH travels through the bloodstream to the adrenal cortex.
4. The adrenal cortex releases **cortisol**, an important stress hormone.

Cortisol helps regulate metabolism, energy availability, immune activity, and the body's response to stress.

Stress-related signaling through this system can also influence gastrointestinal function.

During prolonged or repeated stress, changes in neuroendocrine signaling may contribute to altered gut motility, sensitivity, and digestive symptoms.

However, cortisol isn't solely responsible for the stomach sensations associated with anxiety.

The nervous system, stress hormones, and digestive tract work together through multiple overlapping pathways.

## Why Does Your Stomach Hurt More When You're Already Anxious?

Sometimes anxiety doesn't just change digestion. It also changes how strongly your brain interprets sensations coming from the gut.

This involves a concept called **visceral hypersensitivity**.

Visceral hypersensitivity occurs when the nervous system becomes unusually sensitive to stimulation from internal organs.

For example, the normal stretching of your stomach after eating may feel more uncomfortable when you're highly anxious.

This doesn't mean the pain is imaginary.

It means the nervous system is processing signals differently.

Brain regions involved in attention, emotion, and pain processing can influence how intensely gastrointestinal sensations are experienced.

This is especially relevant to disorders of gut-brain interaction, such as **irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)**, where altered gut sensitivity and brain-gut communication can contribute to recurring digestive symptoms.

However, having stomach discomfort when you're anxious doesn't automatically mean you have IBS.

## Can Your Gut Make Your Anxiety Worse?

The gut-brain axis works in both directions.

Just as anxiety can change digestive function, uncomfortable gastrointestinal sensations can influence emotional processing.

For example, imagine feeling nauseated before an important presentation.

You notice the nausea, become worried about feeling sick in front of everyone, and then feel even more anxious.

That additional anxiety may intensify your gastrointestinal symptoms.

This creates a feedback loop:

**Anxiety -> Changes in gut function -> Increased awareness of stomach discomfort -> More anxiety**

The cycle can be frustrating because each part reinforces the other.

Understanding that these sensations are connected to real physiological processes can help explain why they occur.

## How Can You Calm an Anxious Stomach?

Although you can't always eliminate stress, several strategies may help reduce its physical effects.

### 1. Practice Slow, Comfortable Breathing

When you're anxious, your breathing may become faster or shallower.

Slowing your breathing can help reduce physiological arousal and may make gastrointestinal discomfort feel more manageable.

### 2. Try Gentle Movement

Walking or light physical activity may help some people manage stress and abdominal tension.

Avoid pushing through severe discomfort.

### 3. Maintain Regular Eating Habits

Skipping meals or eating very large meals immediately before stressful events can sometimes worsen digestive discomfort.

Eating familiar foods at comfortable intervals may help.

### 4. Get Adequate Sleep

Sleep and stress regulation are closely connected.

Insufficient sleep can make emotional regulation more difficult and may increase sensitivity to physical discomfort.

### 5. Address the Source of Stress

Relaxation techniques, preparing for stressful situations, talking with someone you trust, or working with a mental health professional can help manage recurring anxiety.

For persistent symptoms, treatments targeting the gut-brain connection may also be helpful.

## When Should You Be Concerned?

Occasional stomach discomfort during stressful situations is common.

However, persistent or severe abdominal symptoms shouldn't automatically be attributed to anxiety.

Consider seeking medical evaluation if symptoms repeatedly interfere with eating, sleeping, school, or daily activities.

Other concerning symptoms include:

- Persistent or worsening abdominal pain
- Repeated vomiting
- Unexplained weight loss
- Blood in the stool
- Difficulty swallowing
- Significant changes in bowel habits

**Sudden, severe abdominal pain requires prompt medical attention.**

Anxiety and gastrointestinal conditions can also occur together, so one doesn't necessarily rule out the other.

## The Takeaway

That sinking sensation in your stomach before a stressful moment isn't just a figure of speech.

It's the result of a complex communication network involving your brain, autonomic nervous system, gastrointestinal tract, and stress hormones.

When your brain perceives a stressful situation, it can activate the sympathetic nervous system and HPA axis, changing how your digestive system moves, responds, and communicates sensory information.

At the same time, your brain may become more sensitive to signals coming from your internal organs, making those butterflies, cramps, or waves of nausea feel even stronger.

So the next time you're nervous before an exam and your stomach suddenly decides to join the conversation, remember:

Your stomach doesn't have to understand what you're worried about to respond to it.

**Your brain has already sent the message.**
